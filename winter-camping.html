Winter Camping: Staying Warm and Safe in the Snow

Winter camping turns familiar trails into a quiet, empty world — no crowds, no bugs, and snow that makes every view feel new. It is also the season where small mistakes get expensive fast. The good news is that staying warm is a system, not a talent: insulation from below, dry layers, enough food, and a plan for water.

Your sleeping setup matters more than anything else. A winter sleeping bag rated well below the expected overnight low is the starting point, but the bag only works if the ground does not steal your heat. Use a sleeping pad with a high R-value, or stack two pads — a closed-cell foam pad under an insulated air pad is a proven combination. Before bed, change into dry sleeping clothes kept in a sealed bag; never sleep in the damp layers you hiked in. A hot water bottle tucked into the foot of the bag adds real comfort on the coldest nights.

Clothing works in layers you can add and remove. A moisture-wicking base layer, an insulating mid-layer such as fleece or down, and a windproof shell handle most conditions. Avoid cotton: it holds sweat, loses its insulation when wet, and dries slowly. Keep a dry set of socks and gloves reserved for camp, and wear a warm hat — a surprising amount of heat escapes from an uncovered head.

Eating and drinking take more effort in winter. Your body burns extra calories just staying warm, so bring more food than you would in summer, and favour warm, calorie-dense meals. Melting snow for water takes time and fuel, so carry a stove with plenty of fuel and start melting snow early. Keep your water bottle upside down inside your sleeping bag overnight so the opening does not freeze shut, and never eat yellow snow or unmelted snow as a water source — it lowers your core temperature.

Plan for shorter days and longer nights. Set up camp with daylight to spare, because pitching a tent and cooking by headlamp in wind and snow is slow, cold work. Check the forecast and avalanche guidance for your area before you go, tell someone your route and return time, and carry a charged phone in an inside pocket where body heat keeps the battery alive. With the right sleep system and a realistic plan, winter camping is one of the most rewarding trips you can take.
