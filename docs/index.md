# Desert Camping: Water, Shade and Heat Safety

Desert camping rewards you with open skies, silence, and stars you will never see from a city — but the desert does not forgive careless planning. The two rules that matter most are water and heat. The National Park Service guidance is simple: carry at least one gallon (about 4 litres) of drinking water per person per day, and drink before you feel thirsty. In hot, dry air you lose water faster than you notice, and by the time you feel thirsty you are already behind. Carry more than you think you need, keep a reserve in the car, and never count on finding water at a desert campsite unless the park says so in writing.

Plan your day around the heat. Hike in the early morning or late afternoon, rest in the shade through the middle of the day, and wear a wide-brimmed hat, loose light clothing, and sunscreen. Heat exhaustion creeps up quietly — heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache — and the treatment is shade, water, and cooling down immediately. If someone stops sweating, becomes confused, or faints, that is heat stroke: call for help at once and cool them any way you can. Children and older campers are at higher risk, so check on them often.

Pick your campsite with the ground in mind. Never camp in a dry wash or narrow canyon bottom: a storm miles away can send a flash flood down a wash with almost no warning, and that is one of the real killers in desert parks. Check the weather forecast and the park's flash-flood guidance before you set up, and if the sky looks threatening upstream, move to high ground. Choose a flat spot with natural shade if you can find it, and angle your tent so the door faces away from the prevailing wind — desert wind picks up dust and can flatten a poorly staked tent in minutes.

Nights in the desert turn cold fast, sometimes by 30 degrees or more after sunset, so pack a warm layer and a sleeping bag rated for the overnight low, not just the daytime high. Keep food sealed and stored as the park requires, and tell someone outside your group where you are going and when you expect to be back — desert cell signal is unreliable at best. With water, shade, and a realistic plan, desert camping is one of the most rewarding trips you can take.
